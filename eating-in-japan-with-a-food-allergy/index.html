Eating in Japan With a Food Allergy: What to Say

On this page

  2. What to say at a restaurant
  3. How restaurants in Japan respond
  4. Reading food labels
  5. If you have a reaction

You can eat well in Japan with a food allergy, but you need to say it clearly, in Japanese, before you order. Fish stock, wheat, sesame and egg turn up in dishes where you wouldn’t expect them, so treat every soup and sauce as unknown until someone checks. Packaged food is often easier than restaurants, because it is required to list the main allergens.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If your allergy is severe, talk to your doctor before you travel and keep your emergency medicine with you at all times.

Where ingredients hide in Japanese food

Japanese food often looks simple, but a lot happens in the stock and the sauces. These are the hidden ingredients to know:

  • Dashi is the stock behind a great deal of Japanese cooking. It is usually made from bonito fish flakes and kelp, and it goes into miso soup, noodle soup and simmered dishes.
  • Shōyu (soy sauce) usually contains wheat.
  • Mirin and cooking sake contain alcohol.
  • Goma (sesame) is common in dressings and sauces.
  • Seafood extracts show up in snacks and seasonings.

One more thing catches many people out: to a lot of cooks, “no meat” doesn’t mean “no fish.” A vegetable dish may still be made with fish stock. Plain grilled or raw food is usually easier to check than a mixed or simmered dish.

What to say at a restaurant

Explaining an allergy through a translation app often goes badly. Staff may nod without understanding how serious it is. A short written card in natural, polite Japanese works much better, and it lets staff give you an honest answer.

The most important thing is to match the message to how serious your allergy is:

  • For a severe allergy, say that even a tiny amount is dangerous, and that stock, sauces, shared frying oil and shared utensils are also a risk.
  • For a mild allergy or intolerance, say that a small amount is fine but you’d prefer it left out.

Don’t use the mild version when your allergy is serious. Staff will take you at your word. Be exact about the food, too. “Seafood” is vague; say whether you mean shellfish only, or fish as well.

How restaurants in Japan respond

Restaurants in Japan don’t have to label allergens the way packaged food does. Many can’t promise that a dish is free of something, and some will politely turn you away rather than take a risk. If staff hesitate and say chotto… (“it’s a bit…”) or muzukashii desu (“that’s difficult”), they are saying no. It’s frustrating, but it’s an honest answer.

A simple way to approach it:

  1. At a chain restaurant, ask for the allergen chart (arerugī hyō). Many chains have one in the restaurant or online.
  2. Somewhere smaller, book ahead if you can and mention your allergy in the booking. Hotel front desks often call restaurants for guests.
  3. When you arrive, show your card before you order, not after the food comes.
  4. If staff answer clearly, order the simplest dish they say is safe: plain rice, food grilled with only salt, or raw vegetables.
  5. If they hesitate, thank them and choose another place. Don’t push them to say yes.

Small family-run restaurants often use sauces they buy in, so they may not know every ingredient even if they want to help.

Reading food labels

Packaged food in Japan has to list 8 allergens, and makers are asked to list about 20 more. Look for a bracket near the end of the ingredient list (原材料名) that often starts with 一部に (ichibu ni, “contains some”), for example （一部に小麦・卵・大豆を含む）. Also look for factory warnings, which say the factory also makes products containing a certain ingredient.

Here is how some common allergens are written, and how labels treat them as of November 2026 (check before you go, as rules can change):

Japanese Reading English On labels
卵 tamago Egg Required
乳・乳製品 nyū / nyūseihin Milk, dairy Required
小麦 komugi Wheat Required
そば soba Buckwheat Required
落花生 rakkasei Peanut Required
くるみ kurumi Walnut Required
えび ebi Shrimp, prawn Required
かに kani Crab Required
ごま goma Sesame Recommended only
大豆 daizu Soy Recommended only
魚 sakana Fish Only some kinds

“Recommended” items may be missing from some labels, so a clean-looking label isn’t a guarantee. Don’t trust the picture on the front of the pack, and don’t assume a product you know from home has the same recipe in Japan. Recipes change, so recheck things you buy often. Rice balls, sandwiches and salads at convenience stores often show allergens as icons on the pack. On travel days, carrying safe snacks from home is the easy option.

If you have a reaction

If you feel symptoms such as your throat tightening, don’t wait to see if it passes:

  1. Use your adrenaline auto-injector (such as an EpiPen) if you have one prescribed.
  2. Call 119 for an ambulance. You can say Kyūkyū desu. Arerugī desu. (“It’s an emergency. It’s an allergy.”)
  3. Show a written note to the people around you and to the paramedics, saying what you’re allergic to and where your medicine is.

Make sure your travel companions know where your medicine is. On flights, keep it in your hand luggage with a doctor’s letter, and check Japan’s rules on bringing in medicine before you travel.

Useful Japanese

  • アレルギーがあります。 (Arerugī ga arimasu.) — I have an allergy.
  • 少しでも食べると危険です。 (Sukoshi demo taberu to kiken desu.) — Even a small amount is dangerous for me.
  • アレルギー表はありますか？ (Arerugī hyō wa arimasu ka?) — Do you have an allergen chart?
  • だしに魚は入っていますか？ (Dashi ni sakana wa haitte imasu ka?) — Is there fish in the stock?
  • 救急車を呼んでください！ (Kyūkyūsha o yonde kudasai!) — Call an ambulance!
