How to Rent an Apartment in Japan as a Foreigner

To rent an apartment in Japan as a foreigner, you find a place through an agent, apply with your residence card and proof of income or enrollment, pass screening by the landlord and a guarantor company, then sign a contract and pay the move-in costs. Expect to pay around four to six months’ rent up front, because several one-time fees sit on top of the first month’s rent. Most people, Japanese and foreign, now use a guarantor company instead of asking a person to vouch for them.

What you pay up front

The move-in bill catches many newcomers out. A place with ¥80,000 monthly rent can come with an estimate of almost ¥400,000 before you get the keys. Before you apply, ask the agent for the shoki hiyō (move-in cost estimate) and get it in writing.

These are the usual items. The amounts are typical ranges as of November 2026, not fixed prices, so check the exact figures for each listing.

Item What it is Typical amount Do you get it back?
Shikikin (敷金) Deposit 0–2 months’ rent Yes, minus repair costs
Reikin (礼金) Key money, a thank-you payment to the landlord 0–2 months’ rent No
Agency fee Paid to the real estate agent Up to 1 month’s rent plus tax (legal limit) No
Guarantor company fee Initial screening and guarantee fee Often 50–100% of 1 month’s rent No
Fire insurance Often required Often around ¥15,000–20,000 for 2 years No
Key change fee New locks for the new tenant Often ¥15,000–25,000 No
First month’s rent Plus the rest of the current month if you move in mid-month 1 month or more No

Compare places by the total, not by the monthly rent. If the total is too high, it’s normal to ask whether the key money or the agency fee can be lowered. It doesn’t always work, but asking is a normal part of the process.

Two costs are easy to miss. Many contracts in the Tokyo area charge a renewal fee (kōshinryō) of about one month’s rent every two years. And a “zero key money, zero deposit” listing can hide a high cleaning fee or a fee for leaving early, so read the contract before you get excited.

The guarantor company

Almost every rental in Japan needs a guarantor (hoshōnin). Today most tenants use a guarantor company (hoshō gaisha). You pay a fee, the company screens you, and it promises the landlord that it will cover the rent if you don’t pay.

Tell the agent at the start that you’ll use a guarantor company, and ask which ones the property accepts, because some landlords allow only one. On top of the initial fee, many companies charge a yearly renewal fee, often around ¥10,000.

Don’t ask a coworker or friend to be your personal guarantor. It carries real legal responsibility, and many people will politely refuse. If one guarantor company rejects you, ask the agent to try another company or another property. One rejection doesn’t mean every company will say no.

Documents you’ll usually need

Have these ready before you start viewing, because good places go quickly:

  • Residence card
  • Passport
  • Proof of income or enrollment, such as an employment contract, payslips or a student ID
  • A Japanese phone number you will actually answer

That last point matters more than it sounds. Guarantor companies and agents often call to confirm details during screening. If they can’t reach you, screening can stall.

Reading a listing

Japanese listings use short codes. A line like “1K, 6.5畳, 徒歩8分, RC” tells you a lot once you know how to read it:

  • 1R is one room with a small kitchen inside it. 1K is one room plus a separate kitchen area. 1DK and 1LDK add a dining (D) or living-dining (L) space.
  • 畳 (jō) is a tatami mat unit. In listings, one jō is at least 1.62 m², so 6 jō is roughly 10 m².
  • 徒歩 (toho) is the walking time to the station, calculated at 80 meters per minute.
  • RC (reinforced concrete) buildings are usually quieter. Wooden (木造) and light steel (軽量鉄骨) buildings often let more noise through.

Look for a “foreigner OK” (外国人可) filter on rental sites, or use an agency that works in English. Some popular listings stay online after they’ve been rented, so always ask whether a place is still available.

Step by step: from viewing to keys

  1. View the apartment. Check what photos don’t show: phone signal, water pressure, sunlight, noise, space for a washing machine, and where the trash point is.
  2. Apply. If you want it, fill in the application (mōshikomi) that day. Saying “I’ll take it” starts the process; it doesn’t mean the place is yours yet.
  3. Wait for screening. The landlord and the guarantor company check you, which usually takes a few days to a week or so. Answer every call, including from numbers you don’t know.
  4. Go through the contract. Before you sign, a licensed agent is required to explain the important points (jūyō jikō setsumei). Ask for the documents a day or two early so you can translate them.
  5. Check the key terms. Contract length (usually two years), whether it’s a regular lease (futsū shakuya, normally renewable) or a fixed-term lease (teiki shakuya, which ends on a set date and may not be renewed), the renewal fee, the notice period (often one month, sometimes two), early cancellation fees and the move-out cleaning fee.
  6. Pay and collect your keys. Move-in costs are usually paid by bank transfer before a deadline. You get the keys on or before the start date.

Avoid applying for several places at once and then canceling the rest. Agents and landlords can see that as unreliable.

After you move in

You usually set up electricity, gas, water and internet yourself. Gas often needs a staff visit to turn it on, and fiber internet can take a few weeks, so book both as soon as you know your date. Within 14 days of moving in, register your new address at your city or ward office, bringing your residence card. On move-in day, photograph every wall, floor and fixture. Those photos help if there is a dispute over your deposit later.

Useful Japanese

  • 初期費用は全部でいくらですか？ (Shoki hiyō wa zenbu de ikura desu ka?) — How much are the total move-in costs?
  • 外国人でも借りられますか？ (Gaikokujin demo kariraremasu ka?) — Can a foreigner rent this place?
  • 保証会社を使いたいです。 (Hoshō gaisha o tsukaitai desu.) — I’d like to use a guarantor company.
  • 礼金を下げてもらうことはできますか？ (Reikin o sagete morau koto wa dekimasu ka?) — Can the key money be lowered?
  • もう一度ゆっくり説明してください。 (Mō ichido yukkuri setsumei shite kudasai.) — Please explain that again slowly.
