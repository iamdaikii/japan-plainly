How to Write Your Name in Japanese: Katakana vs Kanji

To write your name in Japanese, you normally use katakana, the script Japan uses for foreign names. It follows how your name sounds, not how it is spelled, so Emma becomes エマ and Lucas becomes ルーカス. If you want something more decorative for a gift or a piece of art, you can also choose kanji (characters with meanings) to match each sound, but that version is creative, not official.

Japanese has three scripts

Japanese is written with three scripts at once, and each has its own job:

  • Hiragana is used for Japanese words and grammar.
  • Katakana is used for words and names that come from other languages.
  • Kanji are characters that carry meaning. They are used for most Japanese words and for Japanese names.

Your name, as a foreign name, belongs in katakana. That is how it will appear when you check into a hotel or live in Japan. On official paperwork, such as forms at city hall or your residence card, your name is written either in the Roman alphabet or in katakana. It is not written in kanji.

Katakana: the everyday version

Katakana is written by sound. Japanese has fewer sounds than English, so your name is rebuilt from the closest Japanese ones. A few patterns explain most of the changes:

  • L and R both become an R sound. Japanese doesn’t separate the two.
  • V usually becomes B. Victoria is written ビクトリア (bi-ku-to-ri-a).
  • Most consonants get a vowel after them. Japanese sounds are mostly a consonant plus a vowel, so a short English name can turn into several syllables. That is why Christopher, クリストファー, ends up with five kanji in its kanji version.

Because katakana follows pronunciation, start from how you say your name, not how you write it. Say it out loud slowly, break it into small sounds, and match each one. “Chris,” for example, splits into ク・リ・ス (ku-ri-su).

A free katakana converter on your phone can give you a first draft. Then check it against how you actually pronounce your name.

Kanji: the creative version

A kanji name for a foreigner is called ateji. You pick one kanji for each sound in the katakana version and choose characters with good meanings. Emma, エマ (e-ma), can be written 恵茉: 恵 means “blessing” and 茉 means “jasmine.” Japanese people tend to find a well-chosen kanji name charming, which is why it works so well on a card, a notebook or a souvenir.

Here are a few examples of how it maps:

Name Katakana Kanji Meaning of each kanji
Emma エマ 恵茉 blessing, jasmine
Lucas ルーカス 瑠夏寿 lapis, summer, long life
Sarah サラ 紗良 silk, good
Ryan ライアン 来安 arrival, peace
Kai カイ 海 the sea

Keep in mind that a kanji name is for fun and design. You can’t use it on official documents.

How to build a kanji name in three steps

  1. Write your name in katakana first. Every kanji choice depends on this, so get the sounds right before anything else.
  2. Choose one kanji for each sound. Look for characters that have a positive meaning and are common in Japanese names.
  3. Read it back. Check that the meanings sit well together. Names with one to three kanji look the most natural.

Some sounds need a workaround:

  • P sounds have no name kanji. Use the nearest sound instead, usually H or B.
  • Sounds like ティ (ti) and ディ (di) don’t have kanji of their own. Use テ (te) and デ (de) instead.
  • The “n” sound (ン) can’t stand alone as a kanji. Use a character whose reading ends in n, such as 安 (an, “peace”) or 恩 (on, “gratitude”).

Common pitfalls

The biggest risk with a kanji name is picking a character that has the right sound but the wrong meaning. A Japanese reader will notice right away. Some sounds have well-known bad options:

Sound Avoid It means Better choice
shi 死 death 詩 (poem), 志 (ambition)
ku 苦 suffering 久 (lasting), 空 (sky)
ma 魔 demon 真 (truth), 茉 (jasmine)
hi 悲 sadness 陽 (sunshine), 妃 (princess)
do 怒 anger 道 (the way), 努 (effort)

Watch out for combinations too. Two good kanji side by side can spell a real word with a bad meaning. 真空 (ma-ku), a tempting choice for Mark, means “vacuum.” 野良 (no-ra), for Nora, means “stray,” as in a stray cat.

Other mistakes to avoid:

  • Choosing kanji because they look cool. To Japanese readers, the meaning matters more than the shape.
  • Following the spelling instead of the sound. Japanese goes by pronunciation, so build the name from what you hear, not from the letters.
  • Using rare kanji. If most people can’t read the character, the name loses its charm. Stick to kanji in everyday use.
  • Treating the kanji version as your legal name. It isn’t one. Use katakana or the Roman alphabet on forms.

Using your name

Once you have it, there are simple ways to use it. Type the kanji into a design app with a bold font for a phone wallpaper. Copy it large with a brush pen for a handwritten card; for a gift, balance and size matter more than perfect stroke order. Or print the kanji on top, the katakana beneath, and the meanings in small English letters. Vertical writing looks more traditional and horizontal looks more modern. Both are correct.

Useful Japanese

  • 私の名前は＿＿です。 (Watashi no namae wa ___ desu.) — My name is ___.
  • カタカナで書いてください。 (Katakana de kaite kudasai.) — Please write it in katakana.
  • 漢字で書くとこうなります。 (Kanji de kaku to kō narimasu.) — In kanji, it’s written like this.
  • いい名前ですね！ (Ii namae desu ne!) — What a nice name!
