Japan Etiquette for First-Time Visitors: 10 Mistakes

Most Japan etiquette for first-time visitors comes down to a few everyday habits: carry your trash with you, keep quiet on trains, don’t tip, put your money on the tray at the register, and take your shoes off wherever the floor steps up. None of these rules are posted anywhere, because Japanese people grow up knowing them. The good news is that nobody expects visitors to be perfect, and people are usually pleased when you try.

Here are the ten things first-time visitors most often get wrong.

1. Looking for a trash can that isn’t there

Public trash cans are rare in Japan, and that’s deliberate. Many cities removed them because of illegal dumping and other problems, and people simply take their trash home.

Put a small plastic bag in your backpack before you leave your hotel and use it for wrappers and empty cups. Empty plastic bottles and cans can go in the recycling boxes beside vending machines, but only those; don’t push coffee cups or snack wrappers into them. Bins do exist on some station platforms and in coffee chains. Never leave trash on a wall, a bench or in someone’s bike basket just because there was no bin.

2. Taking phone calls on the train

On Japanese trains, people don’t take calls and keep conversations low. Switch your phone to silent before you board (in Japan this is called “manner mode”), use earphones for music and videos, and if someone calls, send a text saying you’ll call back. If it’s urgent, get off at the next stop and call from the platform. In a silent car, everyone can hear you.

3. Standing on the wrong side of the escalator

It depends on the city. People usually stand on the left in Tokyo and on the right in Osaka, and other cities, such as Kyoto, can go either way. Copy the person in front of you. Officially, you’re supposed to stand still on both sides, and Saitama Prefecture and Nagoya City have passed rules asking people not to walk on escalators. Keep your suitcase in front of or behind you, not beside you.

4. Cutting the line on the platform

Platforms have floor markings showing where the train doors will stop and where to line up. Join the back of the line, step to the side of the door when the train arrives, and let everyone get off before you get on. Once inside, move all the way in.

5. Getting stuck at the ticket gate

Suica is a rechargeable transit card, similar to London’s Oyster card. If a gate beeps and shuts on you, it’s usually because your balance is too low, or because the card has no record of where you got on. Step back and use the Fare Adjustment machine just before the gates, or show your card at the staffed window.

To avoid it:

  1. Keep a buffer on your card. ¥1,000 or more is safe.
  2. Use the same card, or the same phone, to enter and exit.
  3. Don’t tap your whole wallet if it holds more than one IC card.
  4. Hold the card flat on the reader until it beeps, rather than tapping quickly.
  5. Use a gate without a red ✕ sign; that one is for people coming the other way.

6. Leaving a tip

Japan basically has no tipping culture. Pay what’s on the bill and take all your change. To say thanks, say gochisōsama deshita as you leave.

Two things aren’t tips. Upscale restaurants and hotels may add a service charge, often 10% but varying by place. And at an izakaya (a casual Japanese pub), a small dish you didn’t order, called otoshi, is a kind of table charge, usually around ¥300–500. If you want to give something extra at a ryokan (traditional inn), put the money in an envelope; handing over bare cash feels awkward in Japan. These amounts are as of November 2026, so check before you go.

7. Handing money straight to the cashier

If there’s a small tray by the register, put your money on it. It lets the cashier count clearly without anyone’s hands touching, and it isn’t rude in any way. Your change may come back on the tray or in your hand; both are normal. Many shops now have cash machines at the register, so if the cashier points at one, put your money in there. Wait for your change and receipt before walking off.

8. Keeping your shoes on

The simple rule is “step up, shoes off.” Take your shoes off wherever the floor steps up and always before tatami (woven straw mats). Look for a step, a shoe shelf or a row of slippers at the entrance. Common places include ryokan, guesthouses and Japanese homes, raised seating areas with low tables in traditional restaurants, inside temple buildings and some castles, and raised fitting rooms in clothing shops. Line your shoes up with the toes pointing out, and take slippers off too before stepping onto tatami.

In ryokan and homes, the toilet often has its own slippers. Wear them in the toilet and leave them there when you come out. Wear shoes that slip off easily, and socks without holes.

9. Eating while walking

People in Japan usually stop and eat close to where they bought the food. Some places officially ask visitors not to eat on the move: Kamakura, a popular day trip from Tokyo, has a rule asking people not to eat while walking in crowded spots (a request with no fine), and Kyoto’s manners posters carry the same message. Eat at the stall or in the shop’s eating space, then hand the stick or container back, since their bin is often the only one nearby. On local trains, don’t eat. Long-distance trains like the Shinkansen are the exception, where a station boxed lunch (ekiben) is part of the trip.

10. Clapping at a temple

At a Shinto shrine, the basic flow is: rinse your hands, make an offering, then bow twice, clap twice and bow once. At a Buddhist temple, you don’t clap. Shrines usually have a torii, a large gate made of two posts and two crossbeams, often painted orange-red.

At the water basin (temizuya), hold the ladle in your right hand and pour water over your left. Then switch hands and rinse your right. Rinsing your mouth from your cupped hand is optional; never put the ladle to your lips. At the main hall, drop a coin gently into the offering box (don’t toss it from a distance), and ring the bell if there is one. Then bow twice, clap twice, put your hands together to make your wish, and bow once more. Many people offer a ¥5 coin, because go-en sounds like the word for good fortune in relationships. Some shrines do things differently, so follow any sign at the hall.

Useful Japanese

  • すみません (Sumimasen) — Excuse me / Sorry / Thank you
  • ごちそうさまでした (Gochisōsama deshita) — Thank you for the meal
  • すみません、ゴミ箱はありますか？ (Sumimasen, gomibako wa arimasu ka?) — Excuse me, is there a trash can?
  • 靴は脱いだほうがいいですか？ (Kutsu wa nuida hō ga ii desu ka?) — Should I take my shoes off?
  • ここで食べてもいいですか？ (Koko de tabete mo ii desu ka?) — Is it OK to eat here?
