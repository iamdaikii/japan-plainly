Lost Something in Japan? Trains, Taxis and Police Boxes

If you lose something in Japan, report it the same day: at the station office if it was on a train, to the taxi company if it was in a cab, and at a koban (police box) if it was anywhere else. Lost items are usually handed to the nearest staff member or police box and sent on to a lost-and-found center after a few days, so the sooner you report it, the easier it is to match. If cards or a phone are missing, freeze them right away rather than waiting to see if they turn up.

Procedures vary by city, railway and company. If staff or the police tell you something different from what’s here, follow what they say.

The first hour

Japan has a reputation for returning lost things, and many items really are handed in. Whether you get yours back mostly depends on how quickly you report it and how clearly you describe it.

  1. Write down the last time and place you definitely had it. Be as exact as you can.
  2. Decide where to report. On a train, bus or taxi, go to the transport company first. On the street, in a shop or in a park, go to the nearest koban.
  3. Freeze any cards and lock your phone now. You can always unfreeze or reissue a card later.
  4. Prepare a description. Color, brand, size, and what was inside. “Black wallet” matches hundreds of items; “black leather wallet with two bank cards and a hotel key card inside” is much easier to match.

Don’t wait a day to see whether it turns up. Report it the same day.

Lost on a train or at a station

If the doors close and your bag is still on the rack, go straight to the station office (eki jimushitsu) or the staffed window beside the ticket gates. Staff can often contact the train or the stations further down the line. Tell them:

  • The line and the direction the train was going
  • The time it left
  • The car number, and where in the car you left the item (rack, seat)
  • What the item looks like

If you can’t wait around, ask them to call you and leave your number. If nothing turns up the same day, check the railway’s lost-and-found page or call center the next day. Each railway runs its own, including JR, Tokyo Metro, Toei and the private lines. On lines shared by more than one company, check which company actually ran your train before you call. Don’t ride to the end of the line to look for it yourself.

Items are kept at the station for a short time and then sent to the railway’s lost-and-found center. If you also file a report at a koban, the police system can match items later.

Lost in a taxi

Contact the taxi company. This is why it’s worth asking for a receipt every time you take a taxi in Japan: it shows the company name, its phone number and often the car number. If you paid by card or through an app, check the app history for the company and car.

When you call, give the time, where you were picked up and dropped off, and the car number if you have it. If you booked through a taxi app, use its lost item function. If you don’t know which company it was, report it at a koban. In big cities, taxi companies hand found items to the police. Don’t wait for the driver to find you; they have no way to reach you.

Reporting at a koban

A koban (交番) is a small neighborhood police box. You’ll often spot it by its red light and an officer standing at the door. It isn’t only for crimes. Walking in to report a lost item is completely normal, and officers handle it every day. A translation app is fine.

  1. Find the nearest koban on a map app by searching “koban” or 交番.
  2. Tell the officer what you lost, and answer their questions about when, where, what it looks like and what was inside.
  3. Show ID if you have it, and give a phone number and address. Visitors can give their hotel.
  4. Ask for the report number (juri bangō) and write it down. You’ll need it for every follow-up call.

Many prefectures also let you report online. In Tokyo, search for the Tokyo Metropolitan Police lost-and-found site.

Cards, phone and passport

Bank and credit cards: freeze each one through your bank’s app or phone line. Keep those numbers somewhere other than your wallet and your phone.

Transit cards: if your Suica or PASMO is registered in your name, or is a mobile card on your phone, you may be able to stop it and move the balance at a station. An unregistered card can’t be stopped.

Your phone: use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device, lock it and display another contact number. Then call your phone company to suspend the line.

Your passport: you need a police report from Japan and a new passport or emergency travel document from your own country’s embassy or consulate. Report it at a koban first, because the embassy will usually ask for the report number. Then book an embassy appointment, and tell your airline if your flight is soon. Residents who lose a residence card also report it to the police first, then apply for a new card at their regional immigration office within 14 days.

Picking it up

When your item is found, the police or the railway will contact you or tell you where to collect it. Answer calls from unknown numbers in the days after you report something. If the caller speaks fast Japanese, ask them to speak slowly or send a text, or have a Japanese-speaking friend call back for you. Bring ID and your report number when you collect. The finder may be entitled to a reward of 5% to 20% of the item’s value, though many finders give that up. If you’re unsure what to do about it, ask the staff.

Useful Japanese

  • 財布をなくしました。 (Saifu o nakushimashita.) — I lost my wallet.
  • 電車に忘れ物をしました。 (Densha ni wasuremono o shimashita.) — I left something on the train.
  • 受理番号を教えてください。 (Juri bangō o oshiete kudasai.) — Please give me the report number.
  • 領収書をください。 (Ryōshūsho o kudasai.) — A receipt, please.
  • ゆっくり話してください。 (Yukkuri hanashite kudasai.) — Please speak slowly.
