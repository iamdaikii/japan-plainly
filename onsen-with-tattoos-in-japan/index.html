Onsen With Tattoos in Japan: How to Check, What to Ask, Where to Go

You can go to a hot spring in Japan with tattoos, but not to every one. Many onsen still refuse tattooed guests, because tattoos have long been linked with organized crime in Japan. Most local sentō (neighborhood bathhouses) let tattooed guests in, a growing number of onsen allow small tattoos covered with a sticker, and some allow tattoos completely. The rule belongs to each bath, so you have to check each place, on its own website, before you go.

Rules change. If a bath’s website or staff tell you something different from what’s here, follow them.

The three answers you’ll find

When you look up a bath’s tattoo rule, it usually falls into one of three groups:

Rule What it means for you
Tattoos OK Go in as you are
OK if covered with a cover sticker Cover each tattoo with a skin-colored patch
No tattoos at all Choose another bath, a sentō, or a private bath

The second group is the one most visitors miss. A small tattoo on your wrist or ankle may be fine if you cover it, while a large back piece won’t be.

Reading the rule in Japanese

Many bath websites only state the tattoo rule in Japanese, often in the “notes” or “rules” section near the bottom. Search the page (Ctrl+F or “Find on page”) for these words:

Japanese Reading Meaning
入れ墨 irezumi Tattoo (the traditional word)
タトゥー tatū Tattoo
カバーシール kabā shīru Cover sticker
お断り okotowari Not allowed / we must refuse
貸切風呂 kashikiri buro Private bath you reserve

If you see 入れ墨 or タトゥー next to お断り, tattoos are not allowed. If you see カバーシール, look at the sentence around it: it usually says tattoos are fine if you cover them, sometimes with a size limit.

Don’t rely on an old blog post or a list of “tattoo-friendly onsen” from a few years ago. Rules change, so the bath’s own site is the one to trust.

What to ask if the website is unclear

If you can’t find the rule, send a short message or email before you go, or ask at the front desk. These two questions cover almost every case:

タトゥーがありますが、入浴できますか？

Tatū ga arimasu ga, nyūyoku dekimasu ka? — I have a tattoo. Can I use the bath?

シールで隠せば大丈夫ですか？

Shīru de kakuseba daijōbu desu ka? — Is it OK if I cover it with a sticker?

Showing the Japanese on your phone works well at a busy front desk. Staff can answer with a simple yes or no, or point at a sticker they sell.

Small tattoos: bring cover patches

If your tattoo is small, buy skin-colored cover patches at a drugstore or online before your trip. Then you’re ready for any bath whose rule is “OK if covered”.

Don’t hide a tattoo with your hand or a towel and hope no one notices. If staff see it and ask you to leave, it’s embarrassing for everyone.

Large tattoos: three good options

1. A sentō

A sentō is a neighborhood bathhouse with heated tap water. Most sentō let tattooed guests in. They’re cheap and simple: in Tokyo the adult price is set by the prefecture and is usually around ¥500 to ¥600. Many open in the afternoon and close late at night. It isn’t a resort hot spring, but that’s the charm.

Don’t confuse a sentō with a super sentō, a large bath complex with many pools, a sauna and a restaurant, usually around ¥1,000 to ¥3,000. Those have their own rules, so check them like an onsen.

2. A tattoo-friendly onsen

Some onsen state clearly that tattoos are welcome. Search for the town name plus “tattoo OK”, then confirm on the bath’s own website before you go.

3. A private bath

A private bath, called kashikiri buro (貸切風呂) or kazoku buro (family bath), is one you reserve for a time slot and use with only your group. It’s the easiest answer for tattoos, and it’s also good if you’re traveling as a couple or don’t want to bathe naked with strangers. Many ryokan also offer rooms with their own outdoor bath.

  • Search for the hotel name plus “private onsen” or “貸切風呂”.
  • Check how to book. Some take reservations at check-in, some online, and some are first come, first served.
  • Note the time limit, often 40 to 60 minutes. Popular slots fill up early in the evening.

貸切風呂を予約したいです。

Kashikiri buro o yoyaku shitai desu. — I’d like to book the private bath.

The usual bath rules still apply in a private bath: wash before you get in, and keep your towel out of the water.
